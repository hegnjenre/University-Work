What's so bad about dark data?

All data gathered, whether by social media companies, businesses, or through research, must be stored somewhere, and the amount to be stored only increases every year.

The Journal of Business practices found that: "at least 2.5 quintillion bytes of data are produced every day," 55% of this being just dark data (Jackson, T.W. and Hodgkinson, I.R. (2023)). Forbes, too, has found that up to a third of all data produced by businesses is ROT (Redundant, Obsolete, or Trivial) (Jelley, S (2023)).

This suggests that around 88% of all data being stored is unused, unnecessary, or forgotten. (Jackson, T.W. and Hodgkinson, I.R. (2023-24)). Data centres create 2.5% of mankind's global carbon emissions, meaning that dark data alone contributes to 2% of our annual CO2 output, just 0.1% less than the entire aviation industry - with 2.1% (Hodgkinson, I.R. and Jackson, T. (2022)).

How is dark data created?

In business, ROT can be created in multiple ways. Jackson and Hodgkinson (Jackson, T.W. and Hodgkinson, I.R. (2023)) laid out three scenarios in which ROT could be created during the business process:

  1. When information isn't socialised: Socialisation is the process in which information is shared between employees so that it can be refined by different viewpoints and levels of knowledge on the subject. Without this, a 'knowledge bubble' is created, where knowledge isn't changed upon new information being released, and isn't challenged by anyone who already knows better within the business. This will lead to employees not sharing findings they believe are already well-known.


  2. When information is socialised, but isn't internalised: If information is socialised but isn't recorded properly - internalised - then it will be forgotten or lost, even if it would otherwise be useful. This information could be deemed useful, but not important enough to properly label and record its existence, leading to the data becoming lost within storage or overall forgotten.


  3. When information is internalised, but is unused: Internalised data can still be lost over time. If the data is largely unused by the business, it may be forgotten by its employees and, upon a system upgrade or general bad categorisation, may be lost within data storage. A change in staff could also lead to data being forgotten, as new employees may not have been informed of the data by the previous employees.

Why is it dark?

Dark data is a coloquial term encompassing two similar concepts:

  • Redundant, Obsolete, or Trivial data (ROT): This type of data is formed when businesses or research insitutes store data without the intent to use it later, leaving it to be forgotten. Alternatively, it could be stored with the intent to use it later, but the opportunity never arises, or its usefulness becomes redundant, leaving it unused in storage.


  • "True" dark data: This is data that is potentially useful - like for a user survey - but it sits unused in company's data storage. Social media often produces a lot of this type of data - things like user location, interactions, searches, etc, that could potentially be collected for analysis, but lies dormant in storage.

In general, it is known as dark data because the data is left unused within a database or cloud warehouse.
