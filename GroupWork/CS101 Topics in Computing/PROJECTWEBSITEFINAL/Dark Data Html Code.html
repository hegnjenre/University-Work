AI Overview

AI is changing our life day by day. AI has made a massive impact in many fields such as health, finance, transport, and entertainment. This drastic advancement has come at a price, and this price is usually not acknowledged. The cost for keeping AI up and running has been the environment. In other words, the more dependent we become on AI systems, especially on large-scale machine learning AI, the greater the energy consumption and hence carbon emissions that start to grow with it. In this paper, I investigate some of the negative impacts of AI in terms of energy consumption,carbon footprint in AI models, and I will also discuss some positives.

Energy Consumption

One of the biggest issues of AI in terms of the environment is the sheer amount of energy that it consumes, in particular deep learning-based AI models which need extremely high power for training.This training is carried out on giant data centers fitted with several thousand graphics processing units among other specialized technology(Mark Labbe, 2021). Training may take days, weeks, sometimes even months, which is why a lot of energy is used (Mark Labbe, 2021). For example, OpenAI's GPT-3 was trained on 45 terabytes of data, while its Nvidia equivalent MegatronLM was trained using 512 V100 GPUs for nine days, A single V100 GPU uses about 250 watts, so that makes for a 128-kilowatt load for the 512 GPUs, this resulted in 27,648 kwh of energy being used over the nine days, or about the amount of energy three average US households consume in a year, estimated at 10,649 kwh per year (Mark Labbe, 2021). This entire process is extremely energy-intensive which is why it is a direct contributor to global warming. As time goes on more complex AI systems will be demanded meaning an increase in energy consumption (Payal Dhar, 2020). In an analysis presented by Dario Amodei and Danny Hernandez of OpenAI in 2018 states, "the computational power in training big AI models has been doubling roughly every 3.4 months since 2012 (Payal Dhar, 2020).

Carbon Footprint

The other problem would be the carbon footprint generated by the AI models. Data centers are amongst the large-scale carbon emitters where AI training is taking place (A.Shaji George et al, 2023). These centers require not only vast amounts of electricity but also cooling systems to prevent the hardware from overheating, these cooling systems are run by water, this is known as a water footprint (A.Shaji George et al, 2023).The water footprint of AI models refers to the total water used during their production, operation, and maintenance, making direct and indirect components (A.Shaji George et al, 2023). Direct water use includes water for cooling data centers and producing microchips, while indirect is the water required to generate electricity for powering these data centers (A.Shaji George et al, 2023). The water footprint can vary significantly based on the model's complexity and the size of the data center, For instance, OpenAI's GPT-3 model reportedly consumed around 700,000 liters of water during its training phase (A.Shaji George et al,. 2023,) further increasing their impact on the environment. A recent study by Cornell University scientists found that training large language models (LLMs) like GPT-3 consumed enough electricity to produce 500 metric tons of carbon, or 1.1 million pounds. This is roughly equivalent to burning coal for 10 continuous hours at a a typical coal-powered plant, which burns about 2.7 million pounds of coal in 24 hours. Moving on to CO2 production, researchers have estimated energy consumption by examining the specialized hardware required for models like GPT-3 and GPT-4, focusing solely on the energy used by this hardware excluding servers and other infrastructure scientists estimate that training an LLM generates around 626,000 pounds of carbon dioxide (Monica de Bolle, 2024).
