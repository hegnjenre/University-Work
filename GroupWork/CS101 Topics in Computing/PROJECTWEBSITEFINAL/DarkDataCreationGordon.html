Through Emails

Our emails are one of the biggest producers of dark data that we directly interact with and contribute to. Emails, especially those with attachments, can create up to 20 kilograms of carbon emissions per year for the average person to keep in their inbox (Brave and Heart.(2023)). Emails also tend to stack up over time, meaning that the longer each person receives emails, the more storage in the cloud they need to store it all. It is estimated that there were around 333 billion emails sent around the world per day in 2022. Since then, the number of emails we send has only increased, 347 billion in 2023, and 361 billion in 2024, so far (Laura Ceci (2023)).

At that rate, we have sent and received almost 380 trillion emails since the beginning of just 2022. At around 75 kb on average per email, that's 28500 petabytes of data being used to store just the emails in our inboxes, since 2022.

Through Emojis

Even something as insignificant as emojis in our text messages can use up incredible amounts of data. In modern phones, a single character (letter) takes up 1 byte of data, while an emoji takes up 4. So, every emoji takes up about as much data as 4 letters in a message (Ibm.com (2023)). This doesn’t sound like a lot, but given the amount of emojis sent by people every day, it adds up.

On average, around 10 billion emojis are sent per day around the world (embryo.com. (2023)), taking up 40 billion bytes (40 Gb) of data to store in texting apps and websites daily. This is all on top of the data used to just store the text within these messages, overall adding up to potentially hundreds of gigabytes of data uploaded to the cloud per day, unused past the first read and becoming dark data within minutes of their original use.

Through Photos

We also tend to take a lot of photos on our mobile phones throughout our lives, most of these photos will end up as dark data, as we quickly forget about them. The average user will store around 2800 photos in their phone’s camera roll, each photo has a file size of between 3 and 5 megabytes depending on what it is, and this file size only increases as new phones with new camera technology are released (Woolf, M. (2023)).

So, there is about 11.2 gigabytes of storage used just to store photos per person on average and around 6.7 billion people have a mobile phone. Meaning 75 billion gigabytes - 75000 petabytes - of data stored is just photographs taken from mobile devices on average, most of which are no longer being used. The advent of cloud storage services - like Apple iCloud, or Google Drive - for mobile devices means that for almost everyone, these photos will also be backed up on cloud storage. Stored as dark data in data warehouses around the world, this massive storage requirement needlessly adds to the power drain of these warehouses.
